SECTION 01

ISO Fundamentals — "The Basics"

Before auditing any standard, I needed the mechanics of how ISO standards, certification, and audits actually work. Because 42001 uses the same harmonized structure as 27001, this foundation transfers almost intact.

How standards get made

International standards are written by technical committees of subject-matter experts from industry, government, and academia. AI standards come from the joint committee for information technology (JTC 1) and its subcommittee for artificial intelligence, SC 42 — the same committee that produced the AI vocabulary (22989) and AI risk-management guidance (23894). Every modern management-system standard shares the same Plan‑Do‑Check‑Act rhythm and the same clause skeleton, so the effort I spent learning 27001's spine transferred directly to 42001; only the discipline on top is new.

Certification vs. accreditation

  • Certification — a certification body (CB) checks that an organization's AIMS conforms to 42001 and issues the certificate.
  • Accreditation — an accreditation body (AB) checks that the CB itself is competent to run those audits. For an AIMS the governing document is ISO/IEC 42006 (the AI analogue of 27006), read alongside 17021‑1.

The chain of trust is AB → CB → organization, and ISO itself certifies no one. Accredited certificates are recognized internationally through the IAF's mutual‑recognition arrangement; an unaccredited certificate can be rejected by a customer, forcing a costly re‑audit. Because AIMS is the newest discipline, the accreditation infrastructure under 42006 matured through 2024–2026.

The multi-year audit cycle

PhaseWhat happens
Stage 1 (initial)Readiness / documentation review — is the AIMS designed? Scope, AI policy, AI risk assessment, the AI system inventory, the impact assessments, and the Statement of Applicability. Findings here are usually areas of concern, not nonconformities.
Stage 2 (initial)Is the AIMS implemented and effective? Evidence, interviews, sampling across the AI lifecycle. Passing both stages earns a 3-year certificate.
Surveillance (Yr 1 & 2)Annual checks (partial scope) that conformity is maintained as models and data change.
Recertification (Yr 3)A whole-AIMS reassessment before the certificate expires; a pass renews it for another three years.

Findings: getting the language right

  • Nonconformity (NC) — a requirement isn't met. Minor = an isolated lapse that doesn't undermine the system; Major = a gap that raises significant doubt the AIMS can govern AI responsibly (or the total absence of a required process).
  • Correction fixes the immediate symptom; corrective action removes the root cause so it can't recur. Auditors review the planned corrective action before any certification decision.
  • Opportunity for Improvement (OFI) — not a nonconformity; a weakness that could become one, recorded separately.

Normative vs. informative — the auditor's compass

The single most important distinction for an auditor. Normative criteria are mandatory (the word "shall") — the only thing you can raise a nonconformity against. In 42001, only Clauses 4–10 and the applicable Annex A controls carry "shall"; the Introduction, Scope, Normative references, and Terms are non‑auditable context, and Annexes B, C and D are informative guidance. Informative material (notes, companion standards, "should") supports implementation but can't be cited as a finding.

▹ Use case

A vendor claims a "42001 certificate" issued by a body with no visible accreditation. I'd treat it like any unaccredited claim — confirm the CB's accreditation status under 42006/17021‑1 before relying on it, and be ready to schedule a proper Stage 1 rather than accept the paper.

SECTION 02

ISO 42001 & the AIMS

What the standard is, why the organization's role changes what you audit, and how it plugs into the wider management-system world.

The core idea

ISO/IEC 42001, published in December 2023, is the world's first standard for an Artificial Intelligence Management System — a risk-based governance framework for developing, providing, and using AI responsibly and trustworthily. It applies to any organization that develops, provides, or uses AI systems, regardless of sector or size. It uses the harmonized structure (Annex SL) shared by every modern ISO management standard, which is why once you know the clause spine from 27001, most of it transfers. Crucially, it is not a technical conformity test of a model — it audits the management system that governs AI across its lifecycle.

The organization's role decides what you audit

This is the pivot that has no equivalent in 27001. An organization applies the AIMS to the role it plays toward each AI system:

  • AI provider / producer — develops or supplies the AI. The audit leans on the design/development lifecycle (A.6), data governance (A.7), and documentation for downstream users (A.8).
  • AI user / deployer — uses AI in its operations. The audit leans on responsible-use processes (A.9), intended-use boundaries, human oversight, and third-party/supplier management (A.10).
  • Both — most real organizations. The auditor confirms the role determination itself is documented, because it drives which controls are applicable in the SoA.

How it integrates with other standards

  • 27001 (information security) — protects the systems and data the AI runs on; an AIMS often sits on top of an existing ISMS.
  • 27701 (privacy) — governs the PII used to train and run AI; where personal data feeds a model, the two overlap heavily.
  • 9001 (quality) — the quality-management discipline behind repeatable AI processes.

Because all four share the Annex SL spine, they can be audited as an integrated management system.

The regulatory tailwind

42001 is becoming the recognized way to demonstrate responsible-AI governance and to prepare for regulation. The EU AI Act and the NIST AI RMF point in the same direction — but for the auditor these are context and alignment, not audit criteria, unless a specific legal/regulatory requirement is pulled into the audit criteria for a given engagement.

◆ Auditor takeaway

Keep "the standard" and "certification" straight, and always pin the role: 42001 states requirements; a CB (accredited under 42006) audits an organization's AIMS against them and, if conforming, issues a certificate valid for a three-year cycle. What is applicable depends on whether the org builds AI, uses AI, or both — establish that first.

▹ Use case

A company buys a foundation model and fine-tunes it for hiring screening. It is a user of the base model but a provider of the derived system — so its AIMS must cover both third-party/supplier controls (A.10) and its own development, impact assessment, and monitoring. I confirm the role split is written down before scoping the sample.

SECTION 03

The Clause Spine (4–10)

Clauses 4 through 10 are where every "shall" lives — the auditable heart of the standard. The spine matches 27001, but the evidence wears an AI lens. Items an auditor expects as retained documented information are marked [DI].

ClauseThemeWhat an auditor looks for — AI lens
4ContextInternal/external issues relevant to the AIMS and a documented determination of the organization's role(s) toward AI (4.1); interested parties incl. AI-affected individuals and society (4.2); a documented, justified scope [DI] naming which AI systems and lifecycle stages are covered (4.3); an AIMS that actually operates (4.4).
5LeadershipVisible top-management commitment and accountability for AI outcomes (5.1); an approved, communicated AI policy [DI] (5.2); assigned roles, responsibilities & authorities — who signs off on AI risk and impact (5.3).
6PlanningRisks & opportunities (6.1.1); a documented AI risk assessment process [DI] that considers harms to people and society, not just the org (6.1.2); AI risk treatment + the SoA [DI] (6.1.3); the AI system impact-assessment process [DI] (6.1.4); measurable AI objectives [DI] (6.2); planning of changes (6.3).
7SupportResources (7.1); AI competence — data science + ethics + risk [DI] (7.2); awareness (7.3); communication (7.4); creating & controlling documented information [DI] (7.5).
8OperationOperational planning & control of AI processes incl. outsourced/third-party AI [DI] (8.1); the AI risk assessment performed on schedule/on change [DI] (8.2); the AI risk treatment implemented [DI] (8.3); the AI system impact assessment performed [DI], proportionate to risk (8.4).

◆ The two homes of the AI system impact assessment

The AI system impact assessment (AIA) appears twice in the spine. 6.1.4 requires you to establish the process; 8.4 requires you to perform it. An auditor confirms both — a defined AIA method and completed assessments, each proportionate to that system's risk to individuals, groups and society. Finding one without the other is a classic gap.

▹ Use case

On a Stage 1, I walk the spine top-to-bottom and build an evidence map: for each "shall," which document or record proves it. The AI-specific rows — 6.1.4/8.4 (AIA) and 6.1.2/8.2 (AI risk) — get extra attention because they have no ISMS equivalent and are where design-stage gaps hide.

SECTION 04

Annex A Controls — 38 in nine objectives

Annex A is a reference set of 38 controls an organization considers when treating AI risk. Every control appears in the SoA with a decision to apply or exclude, and a justification. The identifiers run A.2 through A.10 (A.1 is the introduction), so control numbering starts at .2 within each objective.

The role of Annexes B, C and D

Annex A lists the controls; Annex B gives implementation guidance for each; Annex C lists potential AI-related organizational objectives and risk sources (useful input to the risk assessment); and Annex D covers applying the AIMS across domains and sectors. Only Annex A is normative — B, C and D are informative, so I use them to interpret intent, never to raise a finding.

SECTION 05

AI Risk, the Impact Assessment & the SoA

42001 is a risk-based standard, but the AI difference is who the risk is about. The control set flows from AI risk; the impact assessment is the AI-specific centerpiece; and the SoA records the reasoning.

AI risk assessment (6.1.2) — beyond the org

The organization defines and applies a documented, repeatable risk process. The key departure from an ISMS: AI risk must consider harms not only to the organization but to individuals, groups, and society — bias, safety, exclusion, loss of autonomy, misinformation. ISO/IEC 23894 is the companion guidance I benchmark this process against. Risk criteria, consistent method, identified risks, and risk owners all still apply.

The AI system impact assessment (AIA) — the centerpiece

The AI system impact assessment is the artifact with no ISMS equivalent, and the thing an AIMS audit turns on. It lives in two clauses — 6.1.4 (establish the process) and 8.4 (perform it) — and is mirrored in Annex A control objective A.5. Companion standard ISO/IEC 42005 is my benchmark for its depth and content. An AIA describes the system and its intended use, identifies affected individuals and groups, analyses potential harms (bias, safety, privacy, societal), rates likelihood and severity, and records mitigations, human oversight, and residual impact. It is required where AI could affect people and its depth is proportionate to risk — a low-stakes internal tool needs less than an automated decision affecting someone's job or credit.

Risk treatment & the SoA (6.1.3)

For each risk, select treatment options and determine the controls needed, then compare against Annex A to check nothing necessary was missed. The Statement of Applicability records, for every Annex A control: whether it's necessary (and why), whether it's implemented, and — for anything excluded — the justification, tied back to the organization's role. Risk owners approve the treatment plan and accept residual risk.

◆ Auditor tests on the SoA and AIAs

SoA: (1) consistent with the risk treatment plan and the role determination? (2) every inclusion/exclusion justified and traceable to AI risk or a legal driver? (3) does the claimed status hold up in evidence? AIAs: (4) is the process defined (6.1.4)? (5) are assessments actually performed (8.4) for the systems that need them? (6) do they cover harms to individuals and society, with human oversight and residual sign-off?

▹ Use case

An SoA marks A.5 "applicable / implemented," but only one of five in-scope AI systems has a completed AIA, and that one never assesses societal impact. The inconsistency between risk → AIA process → performed assessment → evidence is a textbook audit trail — usually a nonconformity graded on how central the missing system is.

19011 vs 17021-1 — a classic distinction

ISO 19011 gives guidance for auditing management systems and applies to all auditors (internal/first-party, second-party). ISO/IEC 17021-1 states requirements for bodies performing third-party certification — impartiality, competence, and the certification process — and for an AIMS is read with the AI-specific 42006. For certification audits, 17021-1/42006 requirements take precedence; 19011 is still used for guidance.

▹ Use case

Asked "which standard governs your internal audit?" I answer 19011 (guidance) — but for the CB's external AIMS audit, 17021-1 plus 42006 set the rules. Knowing which hat applies keeps findings and expectations correct.

SECTION 07

The Lead Auditor Path

Two things get conflated: the training course (knowledge + exam) and the personnel certification (independent proof of competence and logged audit experience). AIMS is the newest of the three disciplines, so most AIMS lead auditors bring an existing 27001 credential plus AI/ML literacy.

Training vs. certification

  • Accredited training — the Lead Auditor course is typically a 5-day accredited course ending in an exam; the training certificate is generally valid for around three years.
  • Personnel certification / registration — accredited schemes certify people against a competency model aligned to ISO 19011, in grades, each requiring more logged audit experience, maintained through CPD and periodic renewal. The CB itself is accredited under ISO/IEC 42006.

A typical 5-day Lead Auditor course

DayFocus
Day 1Introduction to the AIMS and ISO/IEC 42001 — structure, roles, key concepts.
Day 2Audit principles and preparation / initiation of the audit.
Day 3On-site audit activities — evidence, interviews, sampling the AI lifecycle.
Day 4Closing the audit — findings, nonconformities, reporting.
Day 5Certification exam.

The exam commonly spans seven domains: AI management-system fundamentals; AIMS requirements; audit concepts & principles; preparing a 42001 audit; conducting the audit; closing the audit; and managing an audit programme.

Personnel certification grades

Grade (typical)Professional experienceAudit experience
Provisional / TraineeNone requiredCourse + exam passed; no audits yet
Auditor~2 yrs total (≥1 in AI)~200 audit hours as team member
Lead Auditor~5 yrs total (≥2 in AI)~300 audit hours incl. leading audits

Thresholds vary by scheme and change over time — always confirm against the current scheme documents. Because AIMS is young, "AI experience" is often satisfied through demonstrable AI/ML literacy alongside an existing management-system credential.

The AI-specific competence an AIMS auditor needs

Beyond the generic ISO 19011 model — personal behaviours, audit principles/methods, management-system knowledge, team-leadership — an AIMS auditor additionally needs:

  • AI/ML fundamentals & the lifecycle — inception → design → V&V → deployment → operation/monitoring → retirement.
  • AI risk & the AIA — how to judge an impact assessment's depth and coverage.
  • Data governance & quality — provenance, acquisition, preparation, fitness for purpose.
  • Bias/fairness, transparency, explainability, robustness, human oversight — the trustworthiness properties.
  • Foundation-model & third-party risk — supplier obligations flowed down the AI supply chain.
  • The regulatory landscape — EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF, as context.
▹ Use case

Coming from a 27001 background, I'd map my existing lead-auditor competence onto 19011, then close the AI-specific gaps deliberately — a course plus hands-on familiarity with model documentation, data lineage, and bias testing — and keep an experience log the registration scheme will ask for at renewal.

SECTION 08

The Audit — order of operations

Everything above exists so this can happen well. A certification audit follows this sequence; internal audits use the same skeleton at smaller scale. The AI difference shows up in what you sample and what you trace.

  1. Scoping & role determination — confirm which AI systems and lifecycle stages are in scope and whether the org is a provider, user, or both; establish criteria (42001 + SoA + applicable legal/regulatory, e.g., EU AI Act where relevant); size the audit and decide standalone or integrated with 27001 / 27701 / 9001.
  2. Stage 1 (documentation) — review the AI policy (5.2), AI risk assessment and treatment (6.1.2/6.1.3), the SoA, the AI system inventory, the AI system impact assessments (6.1.4 process + 8.4 results), and data-governance documentation (provenance, quality, preparation); confirm internal audit (9.2) and management review (9.3) have happened; assess Stage 2 readiness.
  3. Planning — build the audit plan (schedule, sites, interviewees, sampling) with sampling justified across the AI lifecycle; prepare working papers mapped clause-by-clause and to the SoA.
  4. Stage 2 (implementation & effectiveness) — opening meeting; sample across the AI lifecycle (model/system documentation, data provenance and quality, verification/validation, deployment controls); test bias/fairness handling, transparency to users, human oversight, and monitoring of deployed models (drift, incidents); assess third-party and foundation-model management; and trace: AI system → risk → impact assessment → controls → monitoring. Test control effectiveness, not just existence.
  5. Findings & closing — evaluate evidence against criteria; classify nonconformities (major/minor) and note OFIs separately; agree findings at the closing meeting.
  6. Reporting & decision — issue the report; the auditee submits correction + root-cause + corrective action; the auditor verifies effectiveness; an independent decision-maker (not the audit team) grants or withholds certification.
  7. Follow-up & ongoing cycle — surveillance in years 1 & 2 (partial scope), recertification in year 3 (whole AIMS), plus special/short-notice audits for major model changes, new AI systems, or incidents.

Nonconformity classification — the core skill

TypeDefinitionTypical AI examples
MajorAbsence or total breakdown of a required process; a gap that raises significant doubt the AIMS can govern AI responsibly; or many minors against one requirement.No AI system inventory; no impact assessments at all; the AI risk process undefined; SoA missing; a control claimed applicable but wholly unimplemented.
MinorAn isolated lapse that doesn't by itself break the system.One AI system missing an impact assessment; a data-provenance record incomplete for one dataset; a single competence record absent.
OFINot a nonconformity — a weakness that could become one, or an improvement idea.Monitoring exists but thresholds under-defined; the AIA template could better capture societal impact.

Common AIMS nonconformities — the AI hot-spots

  • No AI system inventory — shadow AI (tools adopted outside governance) never captured in scope.
  • Missing or superficial AIAs — impact assessments absent, or not proportionate to the risk to people and society.
  • Data-governance / provenance gaps — lineage undocumented, quality not assessed, preparation uncontrolled (A.7).
  • Undefined human oversight — no meaningful human control or escalation for high-impact decisions.
  • Unmanaged third-party / foundation-model risk — supplier obligations not flowed down (A.10).
  • SoA not justified — inclusions/exclusions not traceable to the AI risk assessment or the role.
  • No model monitoring / incident process — drift, performance and incidents not tracked; no incident-communication path (A.8.4).

The 3-part rule for a defensible finding: state the requirement (clause/control), the evidence (what you saw, where, when, sample IDs), and the nonconformity (the specific gap). Don't put the fix in the finding — recommending solutions compromises impartiality. Grade on impact to the system, not on how easy it is to fix.

▹ Use case

Planning a Stage 2, I allocate time to trace two real audit trails end-to-end — for a high-impact AI system, follow it from the risk register through its AIA, the applied Annex A controls, and into the monitoring records — and to reconcile the SoA against operating evidence. That trace is the highest-signal evidence for whether the AIMS actually governs AI.
